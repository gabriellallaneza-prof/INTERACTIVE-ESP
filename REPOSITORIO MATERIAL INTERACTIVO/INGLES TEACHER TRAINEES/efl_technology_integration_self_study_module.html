EFL-Specific Schema Activation

Before exploring theoretical frameworks, consider how language learning differs from content subjects. Reflect on the trigger questions below to evaluate your current beliefs regarding educational technology in English language classrooms.

1 How does technology shift the "input vs. output" dynamic in an EFL class?

Think about traditional text reading versus student-driven podcasts, digital storytelling, or interactive voice synthesis.

0 characters
EFL Expert Perspective: Traditional approaches frequently relegate students to passive receivers of language input (reading static texts, listening to tape recorders). Technology shifts this dynamic from passive reception to active production. It enables instant publishing, speech-to-text pronunciation drilling, and digital multimodal authoring. Learners don't just study language structure—they apply it to construct expressive artifacts for authentic global audiences.

2 What risks are posed by the "novelty factor" of shiny new educational apps in vocabulary acquisition?

Does a gamified word-matching app construct deep lexical knowledge, or is it merely substitution of a paper flashcard?

0 characters
EFL Expert Perspective: Gamified vocabulary drills (like simple multiple-choice word apps) provide engaging reinforcement, but they often only operate at the *Substitution* or *Augmentation* levels (SAMR) or *Passive/Interactive* levels (PICRAT). They risk isolating vocabulary from contextualized communicative use. Effective integration moves past flashcard-style novelty to constructive, authentic projects, such as student-curated digital corpus investigations or collaborative semantic mapping tools.

Triple E Framework Checklist

Engage

Engagement Goals

According to Liz Kolb (2017), engagement should not be mistaken for shallow entertainment. In the EFL context, the chosen tech must support focus directly on target language outcomes with minimal mechanical friction, transitioning them into active, collaborative communicators.

EFL Vocabulary Engagement Scenario:
Instead of solo gamified vocabulary matches, students work in pairs to construct semantic clusters in interactive collaborative maps (e.g., Padlet, Miro), directly negotiating lexical meanings aloud in English to reach co-designed targets.

Comparative Synthesis of Integration Models

Analyze structural differences between each design matrix. Different contexts demand distinct reflective focuses—there is no single "best" model.

Model Primary Focus Strengths in EFL Key Limitations Optimal EFL Use Case
Mishra & Koehler (2006)
TPACK 		Teacher knowledge intersections (TK, PK, CK) Encourages alignment between linguistic goals (CK) and communicative methodologies (PK). Can be complex to assess empirically or self-evaluate on the fly during live lessons. Syllabus structural design; foundational teacher prep curriculum.
Puentedura (2006)
SAMR 		Four-tier taxonomy of tasks (Substitution → Redefinition) Highly intuitive for quickly redesigning reading/writing materials into dynamic, collaborative ones. Hierarchical nature sometimes pathologizes useful substitution tasks (e.g., electronic reading). Rapid redesign of worksheet-based grammar tasks or written assignments.
FCIT (2011)
TIM 		25-cell grid combining integration levels & meaningful environment Extremely robust. Measures authenticity and collaborative dialogue parameters. Resource intensive to implement; high cognitive load for solo teacher reflection. Departmental reviews, curriculum evaluation, and school-wide tech integration.
Hughes (2005)
RAT 		Replacement, Amplification, Transformation Focuses directly on cognitive shifts and procedural efficiency. Lacks explicit, detailed mapping of student-directed agency and creation. Personal reflection on classroom lesson pacing and workflow streamlining.
Kimmons et al. (2020)
PICRAT 		Student relationship (PIC) x Pedagogical influence (RAT) Avoids simplistic linear hierarchies. Highlights creative production in English. Two-axis system requires training to parse accurately in real-time settings. Evaluating active productive skills (speaking, writing) vs receptive tasks.
Kolb (2017)
Triple E 		Alignment of tech tools with curriculum learning goals Combats superficial engagement. Emphasizes real-world extension (bridging boundaries). Slightly less structural focus on specific digital skill scaffolding. Vetting mobile apps, communicative language task planning.
